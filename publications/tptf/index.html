TpTf: Leveraging Global Receptive Fields for Visual Robotic Manipulation with Transporting Transformer Networks

B. Bilgin Senol, Ozgur S. Oguz
Department of Computer Engineering, Bilkent University, Ankara, Türkiye
Paper (soon) Appendix (soon) Code (soon) arXiv (soon)

The Transporting Transformer (TpTf) performing robotic manipulation on the Ravens benchmark using pick-and-place primitives. It predicts useful pick and place positions solely from visual input, without object-level assumptions: (a) align-box-corner, (b) assembling-kits, (c) block-insertion, (d) manipulating-rope, (e) packing-boxes, (f) palletizing-boxes, (g) place-red-in-green, (h) stack-block-pyramid, (i) sweeping-piles, and (j) towers-of-hanoi.

Abstract

Pick-and-place manipulation requires resolving spatial relationships between distant scene regions: the correct grasp location often depends on context far removed from the object itself. Existing approaches such as Transporter Networks address this through convolutional backbones that acquire global context only by depth — stacking many stages to progressively expand the receptive field — and run three independent branches for the pick, place, and crop operations, encoding the scene redundantly at substantial compute cost.

We propose the Transporting Transformer (TpTf), which replaces the three independent branches with a single shared self-attention encoder that computes pairwise interactions across all spatial locations from the first layer, followed by lightweight token-conditioned readout heads for each operation. The shared backbone eliminates redundant scene encoding, reducing total compute from 138.8 to 46.88 GFLOPs, while the shared self-attention representation serves functionally specialized readout heads for pick, place, and crop — properties we analyze through dedicated studies of representation disentanglement and long-range interactions.

We further introduce a multi-task evaluation protocol for Ravens in which a single model is trained simultaneously on all tasks from visual observations alone, without task identifiers or goal conditioning, requiring substantially fewer total training iterations than per-task models. TpTf outperforms Transporter Networks across both single-task and multi-task settings, with the performance gap widening in the multi-task regime, where shared-encoder representations provide greater benefit.

Contributions

  • A Transformer for top-down pick-and-place. TpTf replaces Transporter's three independent CNN branches with a single shared self-attention encoder and lightweight task-specific readout heads. The shared backbone provides a globally receptive field from the first stage, reduces total compute from 138.8 to 46.88 GFLOPs, and outperforms Transporter in both single- and multi-task settings on Ravens.
  • A multi-task protocol for Ravens. A single TpTf model solves all ten tasks simultaneously using only visual input — no task identifiers or goal conditioning — with substantially fewer total training iterations than per-task training. TpTf's advantage over Transporter is larger in the multi-task setting, establishing that the gain is architectural rather than an artefact of the training scheme.
  • Evidence for long-range reasoning and task disentanglement. Two dedicated analyses: a depth-matched stage-wise attention visualization on a sweeping-piles probe that isolates receptive-field effects, and a token-attention analysis on a combined pyramid + assembling-kits scene showing disentangled task-specific readouts from a single shared encoder.

Architecture

The input observation is split into non-overlapping patches and flattened before being processed by a shared self-attention encoder. The encoded features are refined by PatchExpand layers, which reduce embedding depth and increase token count, ultimately generating tokens that represent the pick and place action-value maps. Rotational bins are derived by taking partial crops around the pick coordinate, followed by cross-correlation to obtain a map that correlates placing coordinates with discrete rotation bins.

The shared backbone encodes the scene only once — replacing the three full per-branch passes of the baseline — and three lightweight readout heads (pick, place, crop) specialize the shared representation for each objective. PatchExpand resolves the tension between patch tokenization (needed to keep sequence length tractable) and the per-pixel coordinates that pick-and-place requires.

The savings are structural rather than transformer-intrinsic: Transporter runs three independent encoders that each pay the full convolutional cost, whereas TpTf encodes the scene once. Despite the inherent O(N²) token cost of self-attention, net compute is substantially lower because the backbone runs only once and the readout heads are shallow. TpTf-xs widens the gap further while still outperforming Transporter.

TpTf outperforms Transporter by a larger margin in the multi-task setting than in single-task training, even though both models train under the identical round-robin protocol — directly establishing that the advantage is architectural. Transporter's separate convolutional branches degrade substantially when asked to share a training stream across tasks; TpTf's shared self-attention backbone maintains and extends its single-task lead, and degrades far more gracefully as the switching parameter κ grows (a controlled proxy for the stability–plasticity dilemma in continual learning).

Analysis — Long-Range Interactions

Depth-matched stage-wise dense features for a single step of sweeping-piles — a probe task whose optimal pick location depends causally on a scene element (the target zone) spatially distant from the piles. Top row: Transporter's convolutional pick network. Bottom row: TpTf's self-attention encoder and pick head.

In Transporter, early stages (1–5) respond only to the piles; the target zone first becomes visible around stage 7, and a jointly-localized pile–zone response only emerges at stages 9–11, after enough convolutions have been stacked for the receptive field to span both regions. TpTf instead attends to both the piles and the remote target zone already at stage 1, and the pick head refines this global representation in only two further stages — concentrating its response on the outer pixels facing the target zone, precisely the contact point that displaces the most particles in a single sweep. The architectural difference propagates all the way to the pick policy, not just the intermediate features.

Analysis — Representation Disentanglement

A combined stack-block-pyramid + assembling-kits scene, captured where four pyramid blocks are placed and no kit slots are filled — so the next action could legitimately belong to either sub-task. Rows correspond to the pick, place, and crop stages of a single action.

Sharing an encoder across pick, place, and crop only hurts if the encoder is forced to pick a side — and with token-conditioned readouts it is not. At the pick stage, Transporter activates nearly uniformly over every pickable object whereas TpTf commits decisively to a single stacking block, with the place token already attending to that same block before any cross-branch communication. At the place stage, Transporter fires on the placement regions of both tasks simultaneously — the signature of interference — while TpTf narrows to the elected region. TpTf recovers the non-interference property that Transporter buys through full replication, but with one encoder instead of three.

Real-World Demonstration

To verify that TpTf transfers beyond simulation, we deployed the model on a 7-DoF robotic arm with an overhead RGB-D camera, training on 10 stack-block-pyramid demonstrations collected on hardware and evaluating on held-out arrangements. The model produces stable pick and place coordinates from real RGB-D input and executes the primitive across rollouts.
